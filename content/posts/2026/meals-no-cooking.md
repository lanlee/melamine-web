# Meals No Cooking: What to Know Before You Choose

**Author:** whatever cooker

**Date:** 2026-09-07

**Suggested URL slug:** `meals-no-cooking`

## TL;DR

There are five real ways to get meals with no cooking: assemble ready-to-eat groceries, reheat something frozen, subscribe to prepared meals, order takeout, or let a machine cook raw ingredients for you. They differ most in cost, sodium, and cleanup — here is how to pick between them.

## What "no cooking" means when you are choosing a meal

Almost nobody searching for meals with no cooking means "no heat, ever." They mean: no chopping, no pan to watch, no recipe to follow, and no pile of washing up afterwards.

That distinction matters, because the products marketed at you sit on three different levels:

| Level | What you actually do | Is it cooking? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Assembled cold** | Open, drain, slice, mix | No — but you are still doing prep |
| **Reheated** | Peel a film, press a button, wait | No — and prep is zero |
| **Cooked for you from raw** | Put raw ingredients in, walk away | No — someone or something else did it |

The third row is the one most people have never been offered, which is why the choice usually collapses into "cold plate or delivery." Before you pick a route, it is worth being honest about which part of cooking you are actually trying to avoid — because each route removes a different part of it.

## Five routes to meals with no cooking, compared

| Route | What you do | Time to the table | What you give up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Assemble ready-to-eat groceries** | Drain tuna, slice cucumber, pile a plate | 10–15 minutes | A cooked meal entirely; texture and variety get thin fast |
| **2. Supermarket ready-to-heat** (frozen or chilled) | Microwave or oven, wait | 5–10 minutes | Control over ingredients; most are sodium-heavy |
| **3. Prepared meal subscription** | Open the delivered tub, heat if you want | ~2 minutes | The most money per meal; you eat what someone else decided |
| **4. Takeout or delivery** | Order, wait, unpack | 30–60 minutes typically | The most money and the least nutrition control; packaging |
| **5. A machine that cooks raw ingredients for you** | Load raw ingredients into compartments, walk away | ~10 minutes | Counter space; a one-time purchase decision |

Routes 1 and 2 need nothing but a supermarket. Routes 3 and 4 need nothing but a phone. Route 5 needs equipment — and it is the only one that hands you a hot meal built from ingredients you chose yourself.

A quick way to separate them: **routes 1, 2, 3 and 4 all hand you food somebody else pre-portioned.** Route 5 is the only one where raw ingredients still pass through your kitchen.

## How much time does no cooking actually save?

The honest answer: less than most people expect, unless you also avoid the washing up.

According to the [American Time Use Survey](https://www.bls.gov/charts/american-time-use/activity-by-hldh.htm), Americans aged 15 and over spend an average of **0.71 hours a day — about 43 minutes — on food preparation and cleanup combined**. That average hides a wide gap: about 31 minutes a day for men and about 55 minutes a day for women.

| Who is doing the cooking | Average time on food prep and cleanup per day |
| --- | --- |
| All persons 15+ | 43 minutes (0.71 hours) |
| Men | 31 minutes (0.51 hours) |
| Women | 55 minutes (0.91 hours) |

*Source: [BLS American Time Use Survey, 2025 annual averages](https://www.bls.gov/charts/american-time-use/activity-by-hldh.htm).*

The part of that figure no-cooking routes fail to remove is cleanup. An assembled cold plate still needs a knife, a board, a colander and a counter wipe-down. A delivered meal still needs its packaging dealt with. What you remove when you stop cooking is the standing-and-watching part — which is real, but it is not 43 minutes.

Whatever cooker was designed around the other half of that number. Raw ingredients go in, and a cooked meal comes out in about 10 minutes, and [each layer washes itself afterwards](https://whatevercooker.com/#design) so there is no sink to face. Ten minutes is inside the time it takes to properly assemble a good cold plate — you are not saving time so much as choosing a cooked meal at the same cost.

## The trade-off nobody mentions: sodium

This is the most useful thing to know before you choose, and it is the thing recipe lists never say.

The average American eats about **3,400 mg of sodium a day**, while the Daily Value is **less than 2,300 mg**. And the source of most of it is not your salt shaker: per the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-education-resources-materials/sodium-your-diet), **over 70% of dietary sodium comes from packaged and prepared foods** — exactly the categories you reach for when you stop cooking.

The pattern repeats at the level of single foods. The FDA, citing the CDC, notes that around **40% of the sodium Americans consume comes from a short list of foods**: deli meat sandwiches, pizza, burritos and tacos, soups, and burgers. Look at that list again — four of the five are the standard answers to "I don't want to cook tonight."

| What you are choosing | Where its sodium comes from | How much control you have |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assembled cold plate | Canned fish, deli meat, dressings, breads | Moderate — you choose each item |
| Supermarket ready-to-heat | The recipe, decided by the manufacturer | Low — you can only compare labels |
| Prepared meal subscription | The recipe, decided by the brand | Low — some publish macros, most do not publish all |
| Takeout or delivery | The kitchen, decided by the restaurant | Lowest — usually no label at all |
| Raw ingredients cooked for you | You, at the point of buying ingredients | Full — nothing is added you did not add |

You do not have to calculate any of this. The [FDA's % Daily Value rule](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-education-resources-materials/sodium-your-diet) is a two-second check on any label: **5% DV or less of sodium per serving is low, 20% DV or more is high.** If a "healthy" ready meal is above 20%, it is not a healthy meal — it is a convenient one.

That is the whole reason we started whatever cooker. [Consumer Reports' no-cook advice](https://www.consumerreports.org/health/healthy-eating/guide-to-making-healthy-no-cook-meals-a1062547635/) is a good plate formula — half produce, one quarter whole grains, one quarter protein — but notice that its recommended no-cook proteins keep sliding back toward cans and packets, and it specifically steers you to *no-salt-added* beans and lower-sodium choices. Raw ingredients are the only version of no cooking where that problem does not exist in the first place. ## What each route costs

Food spending has quietly crossed a line worth knowing about. [USDA Economic Research Service data](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/ag-and-food-statistics-charting-the-essentials/food-prices-and-spending) shows that in 2025 Americans spent **$1.41 trillion on food away from home against $1.10 trillion on food at home** — and away-from-home spending reached 4.9% of disposable personal income, edging past food at home at 4.8%.

| Route | Cost profile |
| --- | --- |
| Assemble from groceries | Lowest per meal; you pay with prep time instead |
| Supermarket ready-to-heat | Moderate; premium over the raw ingredients |
| Prepared meal subscription | High. Serious Eats' testing puts it plainly: the less prepared the meal, the lower the price. 2026 roundups report roughly $8–$18 per serving, often before a shipping fee |
| Takeout or delivery | Highest, and rising fastest; fees and tip on top of the menu price |
| Machine that cooks raw ingredients | Raw ingredients at grocery prices, after the equipment purchase |

The pattern is consistent: **every step you move away from raw ingredients, the price per meal rises and your control over what is in it falls.** No cooking does not have to be expensive — assembling from a grocery list still costs grocery money. It is the routes that promise *zero involvement* that charge you for it.

## Four questions to answer before you choose

Work through these in order and the right route is usually obvious.

1. **Is the problem heat, or is it effort?** If you genuinely cannot or do not want to use heat — a heatwave, a dorm, an office, a power cut — you need routes 1 and 3. If your real objection is the chopping and the washing up, no-cook food only avoids the heat and leaves you with the prep.
2. **Who is deciding what is in it?** If you have a health target — protein, fibre, vitamin D, blood pressure — you need to be the one choosing ingredients. That points to routes 1 and 5 and away from 3 and 4.
3. **How many meals a week is this?** A subscription or delivery habit that covers ten meals a week is a very different financial decision from one that covers two.
4. **What happens to the dishes?** Ask it directly. A route that saves you 20 minutes of cooking and gives you 15 minutes of cleanup and packaging has saved you five.

## Our take: the missing middle

The market for meals with no cooking is split into two camps. "You cook it" — recipe kits, meal plans, recipes — assumes you have the time and the will. "Someone else cooked it" — prepared meals, delivery — takes the decision, and the ingredients, out of your hands entirely.

There is almost nothing in between, and the in-between is what people are actually asking for: *a hot meal made from real ingredients, with no work from me.*

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does reheating food count as cooking?

No. Reheating takes no skill, no preparation and no monitoring, which is what people mean by "no cooking." It is a different route from assembling a cold meal, though — reheated food is still food somebody else made and seasoned.

### What is the cheapest way to eat with no cooking?

Assembling from ready-to-eat groceries — canned fish, no-salt-added beans, yoghurt, bread, fruit, pre-cooked grains. It is the cheapest because you are paying grocery prices, and the cost is your own prep time instead of money.

### Are prepared meal delivery services healthy?

Some are, and the label tells you which. Check sodium first using the FDA rule: 5% Daily Value or less per serving is low, 20% or more is high. Many fully prepared meals sit above that 20% line, so it is worth checking before you subscribe rather than after.

### Is it cheaper to buy prepared meals or cook?

Cooking from raw ingredients is almost always cheaper per meal, which is why prepared and delivered food keeps growing as a share of spending. USDA data shows food away from home reached $1.41 trillion in 2025, ahead of the $1.10 trillion spent on food at home.

### How do I eat healthy with no cooking?

Use a plate formula rather than a recipe: half produce, one quarter whole grains, one quarter protein. Then pick your protein with sodium in mind — no-salt-added beans, plain yoghurt, canned fish and hard-boiled eggs beat deli meat and ready-made soups on that front.

### What can I cook without any effort at all?

If you want a hot meal with no chopping, no monitoring and no washing up, you need equipment rather than a recipe. That is precisely what whatever cooker does: raw ingredients in, a fully cooked meal out in about 10 minutes, and each layer cleans itself afterwards.
